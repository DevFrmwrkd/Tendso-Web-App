PEARL 01 / THE FARMPALAWAN WATERS

The oyster does the work. Our job is patience and clean water.

Isla Perla is a South Sea pearl farm off Palawan, and the South Sea pearl is the aristocrat of the pearl world — the largest and among the most valuable cultured pearls, grown in the big Pinctada maxima oyster, which produces the celebrated white and, in its gold-lipped variety, the deep golden pearls the Philippines is particularly known for. The golden South Sea pearl is close to a national treasure here, and Palawan's clean, warm, plankton-rich waters are precisely where it thrives.

The process is slow and unforgiving. A skilled technician grafts a tiny nucleus and a scrap of mantle tissue into each oyster by hand. The oyster is returned to the sea in panels suspended beneath the surface, where it's cleaned, turned and tended for years while it lays down nacre — layer upon microscopic layer — around the nucleus. Typhoons, water temperature, disease and simple bad luck all take their toll. Of every harvest, only a portion are round, clean and lustrous enough to be called fine.

We do everything on one island: grafting, husbandry, harvest, grading, drilling, matching, stringing and setting in our atelier. That means when you buy a strand or a piece from us, we can tell you which farm line it came from and what grade it is, honestly. We sell to collectors and jewellers, supply wholesale, take bespoke commissions, and welcome visitors to see how a pearl is actually made. Nothing about it is quick. That's the entire point.

What is a South Sea pearl, and why is the golden one so prized?

South Sea pearls are cultured pearls grown in Pinctada maxima, the largest pearl oyster, farmed in the warm waters of the Indo-Pacific including the Philippines. Because the oyster is so large, it can accept a bigger nucleus and lay down thicker nacre, which is why South Sea pearls are typically the largest cultured pearls available and have that distinctive soft, deep, almost satiny lustre rather than the sharper shine of smaller pearl types. Pinctada maxima comes in two main varieties — silver-lipped, which produces white and silvery pearls, and gold-lipped, which produces the golden pearls. The golden South Sea pearl is especially associated with the Philippines and highly prized because natural deep-gold colour is genuinely rare, the colour is entirely natural to the oyster rather than dyed, and it takes years of growth to achieve both the size and the nacre depth that give it that glow.

How long does it take to grow a pearl, and why are so few good?

Years — typically several from grafting to harvest for a South Sea pearl, and that's after the oyster itself has been raised to a size where it can be grafted at all. The process starts when a skilled technician surgically inserts a nucleus plus a small piece of mantle tissue from a donor oyster; the oyster then slowly deposits nacre, in microscopically thin layers, around that nucleus. Time is the whole product: thicker nacre means better lustre, more durability and a more beautiful pearl, and it cannot be rushed. In that time, an enormous amount can go wrong. Oysters reject nuclei or die; typhoons, temperature swings, water-quality changes and disease can wipe out a season's work; and even among survivors, many come out off-round, blemished, thin-nacred or dull. Only a fraction of any harvest is fine, and fewer still match into a strand.

How do you grade pearls, and what should I look for?

Pearls are judged on a handful of factors, and understanding them makes you a much better buyer. Lustre is the most important: the depth and sharpness of the glow, which comes from nacre quality and thickness — hold a pearl up and you should see clear, bright reflections rather than a chalky, flat surface. Surface quality is about freedom from blemishes, spots and rings (almost no pearl is perfectly flawless, and minor marks are normal). Shape ranges from perfectly round, the rarest and most valued, through near-round, drop, oval, button and baroque — baroque shapes can be spectacular and are often better value. Size matters enormously with South Sea pearls, since larger pearls are exponentially rarer. Colour is a matter of both rarity and taste. Finally, matching matters for strands and pairs. We grade plainly against these factors and explain them at the counter.

Are your pearls natural or cultured — and is dyed golden a thing?

Our pearls are cultured, and we'd be immediately suspicious of anyone selling quantities of 'natural' pearls today. Truly natural pearls — formed with no human intervention — are extraordinarily rare, effectively historical, and essentially unavailable in the market at any meaningful volume; virtually all pearls sold worldwide, including all fine jewellery pearls, are cultured. Cultured does not mean fake: a human initiated the process by grafting a nucleus, after which the oyster grows a completely real pearl from real nacre over years in the sea. As for colour: our golden pearls are naturally golden, produced by gold-lipped Pinctada maxima, and the colour comes from the oyster itself. Dyed and colour-treated pearls do exist, and cheap 'golden' pearls are often dyed, which is one reason honest provenance matters so much. Because we farm, harvest and grade our own, we can tell you exactly what you're getting.

Can I visit the farm, and can I buy directly?

Yes to both, and we genuinely encourage the visit if you can make it — seeing the panels, the grafting bench and a harvest changes how people understand what a pearl is. Farm and atelier visits let you watch the husbandry work, see graded pearls side by side so the difference between grades becomes obvious rather than theoretical, and choose your own pearl if you're commissioning a piece. We sell directly to individuals — loose pearls, strands and finished jewellery — as well as wholesale to jewellers, designers and international trade buyers. Buying direct from the farm means honest provenance and grading from the people who actually grew the pearl, usually at better value than a long retail chain. If you can't visit, we can send detailed photographs and grading notes and ship securely.

How should I care for pearls so they last?

Gently, and with the understanding that pearls are organic and softer than gemstones. The golden rule is 'last on, first off': put your pearls on after perfume, hairspray, cosmetics and lotions, and take them off before anything strenuous, swimming or showering, because acids, alcohols and chemicals dull nacre over time. After wearing, wipe them softly with a clean, slightly damp cloth to remove skin oils and let them dry before storing. Store them separately from harder jewellery — a soft pouch or lined box — so they aren't scratched, and avoid airtight containers for long periods, since pearls benefit from a little ambient humidity. For strands, have them restrung periodically (we do this for our customers), as silk stretches and soils with wear. Above all: wear them, and bring them back for cleaning and restringing whenever they need it.

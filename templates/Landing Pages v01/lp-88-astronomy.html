FIELD 01 / THE SHOPAPERTURE OVER HYPE

The best telescope is the one you'll carry outside and actually use.

Meridian & Sky is a telescope shop and stargazing school on the edge of the Brecon Beacons Dark Sky Reserve, run by amateur astronomers who got tired of watching people buy the wrong scope. We opened in 2011 because the high-street telescope aisle is a trap — cheap tripods, useless magnification claims, and instruments so fiddly they end up in the loft after one frustrated night.

We sell telescopes, binoculars, mounts and eyepieces we actually use ourselves, and we fit them to the person: how dark your sky is, whether you'll drive somewhere for it, what you want to look at, and how much faff you'll tolerate at midnight in the cold. A good pair of binoculars beats a bad telescope every time, and we'll say so even when it costs us the bigger sale.

And because a scope is only half the story, we teach. Guided stargazing nights, beginners' courses, and setup sessions where we come out with you until you can find the rings of Saturn on your own. We're forty minutes from skies dark enough to see your own shadow by starlight — and we'd rather you saw them properly than squinted at a blur and gave up.

I'm a complete beginner — what should I buy first?

Honestly? Very often a good pair of astronomy binoculars rather than a telescope, and we'll happily sell you the cheaper thing because it's the right thing. Binoculars are forgiving, portable, need no setup, and show you the Moon's craters, Jupiter's moons, star clusters and the Milky Way beautifully while you learn your way around the sky. Once you know a few constellations and what you enjoy looking at, a telescope makes far more sense and you'll choose it far better. If you're set on a scope, we'll steer you to a small Dobsonian reflector — the most sky for your money and simple to use. What we'll always talk you out of is a cheap, high-magnification department-store telescope on a wobbly tripod: they're the single biggest reason people give up on astronomy.

Does magnification matter? The box says 675x!

That number is the biggest lie in the telescope aisle. Useful magnification is limited by the aperture (the width of the main lens or mirror) and by the steadiness of the air, and on most nights anything above roughly 200x shows you a bigger, dimmer, blurrier mess rather than more detail. A telescope advertised at "675x" is telling you it's built for the box, not the sky. What actually matters is aperture (how much light it gathers), a stable mount that doesn't wobble when you touch it, and — more than any spec — how dark your sky is. We'll explain all of this in plain terms and match you to an instrument by what it'll really show you, not by a fantasy number.

Can I really see planets and galaxies, or just dots?

You can genuinely see wonderful things, with realistic expectations. Through a modest scope Saturn shows its rings, Jupiter its cloud belts and four moons, the Moon reveals craters and mountains in breathtaking detail, and many star clusters and brighter nebulae are lovely. Galaxies and fainter nebulae appear as soft grey smudges rather than the colourful Hubble images people picture — your eye can't collect light the way a long-exposure camera can — but seeing the Andromeda galaxy's glow with your own eye, knowing it's two and a half million light years away, is a genuinely moving thing. Dark skies make an enormous difference here, which is exactly why our guided nights are under the Dark Sky Reserve.

What are your guided stargazing nights like?

Small groups, our telescopes, our guides, and some of the darkest skies in Britain. We meet at a site within the Dark Sky Reserve well away from light pollution, let your eyes adapt to the dark, and spend the evening touring whatever the season offers — planets, the Moon, star clusters, nebulae and the Milky Way arching overhead. Our guides will point out constellations, explain what you're seeing through the scopes, and answer every question. We provide the instruments and hot drinks; you bring warm clothes (it gets cold standing still at night, even in summer) and a sense of wonder. They run on clear nights around the new moon for the darkest skies, and they book up — so reserve ahead.

My old telescope doesn't work well. Can you help?

Very often, yes, and it's some of our favourite work — rescuing a scope that's been written off or left in the loft. Most "broken" telescopes aren't broken at all: they need collimating (aligning the optics), a decent eyepiece instead of the toy one supplied, a mount that actually holds still, or simply someone to show the owner how to use it. Bring it in and we'll assess it honestly, sort out what's fixable, and tell you plainly if it truly isn't worth saving. We'll also come out with you for a setup session so you leave able to find things with it, which is usually the real thing that was missing.

Do you do children's parties, groups and gift vouchers?

Yes to all three. Guided nights make brilliant group outings — birthdays, clubs, corporate away-days, and astronomy-mad children (we run sessions pitched for younger stargazers). Gift vouchers for a stargazing night or a beginners' course are one of the best presents you can give someone curious about the sky, and far more memorable than another gadget. We can also bring the telescopes to you for a private group event, sky permitting. Get in touch with your group size and what you're after and we'll put something together — and if the weather scuppers a night, we'll always rebook you rather than send you home with a cloud.

WARP 01 / THE WEAVING HOUSEKALIBO, THE HOME OF PIÑA

Every metre is thousands of knots, tied by hand, one strand at a time.

Hablon Piña Aklan is a handloom weaving house in Kalibo, Aklan — the province recognised as the home of Philippine piña cloth. Piña is woven from the fibre of pineapple leaves, specifically the native red Bisaya variety, and it produces a fabric unlike anything else: extraordinarily fine, slightly stiff, faintly golden, almost translucent, and strong for its weight. It is the cloth of the finest barong Tagalog and Filipiniana, and it has been prized for centuries.

What most people never see is the labour behind a single metre. The leaves are scraped by hand to release the fibres. The fibres are washed, dried, and then — this is the part that stops visitors in their tracks — knotted together end to end, by hand, strand by strand, into one continuous thread long enough to weave with. Only then does the thread go onto the wooden handloom, where a weaver produces a modest length in a day of steady work. Thousands of knots per metre. No machine has ever replaced it.

We've been doing this since 1979, supplying piña, piña-seda (blended with silk) and piña-cotton by the metre to barong ateliers, couturiers, designers and heritage projects here and abroad, and we take commissions for specific weights, widths and hand-embroidered calado. Piña is expensive because it is genuinely, unavoidably slow — and that slowness is exactly the point. Come to the weaving house and watch the knotting; you'll never look at a barong the same way again.

Why it cannot be hurried.

01 / 03

Fibre from red Bisaya leaves.

We use the native red Bisaya pineapple, the traditional variety for piña, and scrape the fibre by hand. The plant and the extraction determine everything downstream — there's no fixing coarse fibre at the loom.

02 / 03

Knotted by hand, strand by strand.

Every continuous thread is thousands of hand-tied knots. It's the slowest, most extraordinary part of the process and the reason genuine piña can't be mass-produced. Nobody has found a shortcut worth having.

03 / 03

Woven on wooden handlooms.

Our weavers work traditional wooden looms, producing a modest length a day. That pace is the craft, not an inefficiency — and it's why each bolt has the subtle life that machine cloth never will.

What is piña cloth, and why is it so highly prized?

Piña is a Philippine handwoven fabric made from the fibre of pineapple leaves — traditionally the native red Bisaya variety — and it's widely regarded as the finest traditional textile the country produces. The cloth is remarkably fine and lightweight, slightly stiff rather than limp, faintly golden or ivory in colour, and close to translucent, with a soft natural lustre that no synthetic imitation quite captures. It's also surprisingly strong for its delicacy. Historically it was the cloth of formal and ceremonial dress, and it remains the material of the very finest barong Tagalog and Filipiniana; a barong in genuine piña is the top of the range, worn for weddings and the most important occasions. Its prestige comes from a combination of that distinctive beauty and the extraordinary labour behind it — piña cannot be mass-produced, and every metre represents an enormous amount of skilled hand work.

Why is piña so expensive?

Because of the hand labour, and the honest answer is that there is no way around it. The process is: pineapple leaves are harvested and the fibres scraped out by hand; the fibres are washed and dried; and then — the step that astonishes visitors — every fibre is knotted to the next, by hand, one strand at a time, to create a continuous thread long enough to weave. That's thousands of hand-tied knots for a single metre of cloth. Only then does the thread go onto a wooden handloom, where a skilled weaver produces a fairly modest length in a full day's work. So a bolt of piña represents days or weeks of several people's highly skilled labour, from the scraper to the knotter to the weaver. No machine has replaced any of the essential steps. When you buy genuine piña you're paying, quite directly, for an immense amount of human time and expertise, and for the survival of a craft that would vanish if that time weren't paid for properly.

What's the difference between pure piña, piña-seda and piña-cotton?

Pure piña is 100% pineapple-leaf fibre — the finest, most translucent, most prestigious and most expensive option, and the traditional choice for a top-tier barong Tagalog or a special Filipiniana piece. It has a characteristic crispness and glow. Piña-seda blends piña with silk (seda), which softens the hand and drape, adds a little more forgiveness in wear and tailoring, and brings the price down somewhat while keeping much of piña's elegance — a very popular middle ground, especially for wedding entourages where several garments are needed. Piña-cotton blends piña with cotton for greater durability, easier care and a more accessible price, making it well suited to barong worn regularly rather than once or twice a lifetime. All three are handwoven here on the same looms; the choice is about occasion, budget and how the garment will be worn. Tell us the garment and we'll recommend honestly.

Can you weave to a custom specification, and do you supply designers?

Yes to both — supplying ateliers, couturiers, tailors, costume departments and heritage projects is a core part of what we do, here and abroad. We can weave to your required weight, width and shade, in pure piña or the blends, and we can add traditional hand-embroidered calado (drawn-thread openwork) and other embroidery in classic patterns or your own design. For any substantial order we strongly encourage sampling first: we'll show or send you a sample of the exact weave and shade so you can handle it and test it against your pattern before committing to yardage, because piña is far too slow and precious for anyone to be guessing. We'll also be straight with you about lead times — handloom weaving sets its own pace and we won't promise a date we can't hit. Bring us the garment, the spec and the deadline as early as you can.

How do I care for piña so it lasts?

Gently — piña is fine, natural and delicate, but cared for properly it lasts for decades and is genuinely heirloom material. Garments should be hand-washed with great care or, more safely, entrusted to a specialist dry cleaner who knows the fabric; never machine wash or wring it. Keep perfume, deodorant and body oils off the cloth by wearing the traditional undershirt beneath a barong. Press with low heat and care, ideally with a cloth between the iron and the fabric. For storage, keep it clean, dry and away from damp — humidity is the real enemy in our climate — and store it flat or on a padded hanger, ideally wrapped in acid-free tissue rather than sealed in plastic. Avoid prolonged direct sunlight, which yellows and weakens it. Handled this way, a piña barong or gown will outlive the occasion it was made for; and when an old piece shows its age, good ateliers can often clean, mend and re-embroider it.

Can I visit and see the weaving?

Yes, and we genuinely encourage it — visits are by arrangement and they're one of the best things about being here in Kalibo. Reading that piña is hand-knotted is one thing; standing beside someone tying thousands of tiny knots to make a single continuous thread, and then watching a weaver work the wooden handloom, is quite another, and it changes how people understand the fabric and its price permanently. Designers who visit tend to leave with a much clearer sense of what they can ask for and why lead times are what they are; students and visitors leave with an appreciation of a craft that very nearly disappeared and survives on a small number of skilled hands. We're happy to host buyers, designers, tailors, students, researchers and interested travellers — just message or call ahead so we can arrange a good time. If you're sourcing seriously, a visit is also the fastest way to work out exactly what cloth you need.

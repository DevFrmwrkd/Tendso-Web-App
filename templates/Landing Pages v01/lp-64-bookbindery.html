QUIRE 01 / THE BINDERYTHE FOLD IS THE SECRET

The fold is the whole secret.

A book that lasts is sewn through the fold of its pages — gathered into signatures, stitched onto tapes, rounded, backed and cased by hand. It's how books were made for five hundred years and why medieval manuscripts still open. The glued "perfect binding" on a modern paperback is neither perfect nor binding; it's a countdown to a loose page.

Signature & Spine works the old way at an English sewing frame in a first-floor workshop on Fossgate. We bind new — journals, guest books, editions, PhD theses that finally deserve boards — and we restore old, from a cracked hinge to a full rebind of a book that's been loved to pieces.

Restoration is a conversation about how far to go. Sometimes the right answer is an invisible repair; sometimes it's leaving the honest wear of a hundred hands and just making it safe to hold. We'll tell you which, and why, before we touch it.

QUIRE 03 / THE METHODFIVE CENTURIES SAY SO

Why sewn beats glued.

01 / 03

Sewn, never glued.

Every binding is stitched through the fold onto tapes, the way a book survives centuries. Glue-only "perfect binding" is a slow goodbye; we don't do it.

02 / 03

Restoration you can trust.

We assess honestly and repair reversibly, using conservation-grade materials. Nothing we do can't be undone by the next binder in fifty years' time.

03 / 03

It opens flat.

A journal you have to hold down isn't finished. Ours lie open on their own, because a book you fight isn't a book you use. The engineering is the point.

Can you save a book that's fallen completely apart?

Usually, yes — a bag of loose pages and detached boards is a rebind, not a lost cause. Bring us everything, including the crumbs; the more original material survives, the more of the book's history we can keep. We'll tell you honestly if it's beyond sensible repair, but that's rare.

What's the difference between restoration and rebinding?

Restoration keeps as much of the original as possible — repairing hinges, reattaching boards, mending pages — so the book stays itself. Rebinding gives a book entirely new covers and structure, which suits a much-loved paperback but not a valuable antique. We'll advise which your book wants; the wrong choice can cost value.

Will restoring an old book ruin its value?

Bad restoration can; ours is conservation-minded, reversible, and honest about wear. For anything potentially valuable we discuss the options before touching it, and sometimes recommend doing less rather than more. If it's a serious antiquarian item, we'll say so and point you to specialist advice.

How long does a commission take?

A hand-bound journal, a couple of weeks. A full restoration, longer — it depends what the book needs and it can't be rushed without cutting corners we won't cut. Theses to a deadline: tell us the date early and we'll plan around it. We'd rather be honest about time than fast and sorry.

Do the journals really open flat?

Completely flat, every page, because they're sewn through the fold rather than glued at the spine. It's the whole reason to have a book hand-bound — you can write across the gutter, lay it open on a desk, and it stays put. Come in and open one; it's the fastest way to understand the difference.

Can I learn to do this myself?

Yes — the evening and weekend courses take you from folded paper to a finished, cased book at the frame. No experience needed, all tools and materials provided. Fair warning: most people leave wanting a bone folder of their own, and it only escalates from there.

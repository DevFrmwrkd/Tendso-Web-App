CUT 01 / THE WORKSHOPDRAWN, THEN CUT

Drawn, then cut. Never printed.

There are two ways to put letters on stone. One is to print a font, cut a rubber stencil, and blast it in with sand — fast, cheap, and dead on the surface. The other is to draw each letter by hand for the words and the stone in front of you, then cut it with a mallet and chisel so the light catches the facets. We only do the second. It's why our work is still crisp when the blasted stuff has gone flat.

The workshop sits on Portland, on top of the quarry that has clad half of London's landmarks. We cut Portland limestone and Welsh slate mostly, drawn from beds a few miles from the banker bench, and we letter by eye — spacing, weight and the tiny optical corrections a stencil can't make.

Much of what we carve marks a death, and we treat that with the time and gentleness it asks for. There is no rushing a headstone and no upselling a grieving family. We'll draw the words, show you the design full-size in chalk, and carry the whole thing at the pace you need.

CUT 03 / THE DIFFERENCEWHY BY HAND

Why cut, not blast.

01 / 03

Hand-drawn for your words.

Every inscription is lettered by hand for its exact text and stone — spacing and weight adjusted by eye. A stencil repeats a font; we compose a piece.

02 / 03

Cut, not blasted.

Mallet and chisel leave faceted, light-catching letters that stay crisp for a century. Sandblasting scours a flat groove that softens and greys within years.

03 / 03

The right pace, especially in grief.

A memorial cannot be rushed and shouldn't be sold. We move at the speed a family needs, show the design full-size before cutting, and never upsell a loss.

Asked at the workshop.

How is hand-carving different from an engraved or sandblasted stone?

A sandblasted inscription is a font printed onto a stencil and blown into the surface — flat, uniform, and quick to soften and grey. Hand-carving draws each letter for your words and cuts it with a chisel, leaving faceted walls that catch light and stay crisp for a century. Stand beside both after ten years and you'll never choose the stencil again.

We've had a death. How does commissioning a headstone work?

Gently, and at your pace. We talk through the words and the stone whenever you're ready — there's no rush and no pressure. We draw the lettering, show it to you chalked full-size, and only cut when you're happy. We handle cemetery and churchyard regulations for you, so the paperwork isn't another weight to carry.

Can you add a name to an existing family grave?

Yes — one of the most careful things we do. We match the original lettering style, size and depth, cut the new inscription on site or in the workshop, and blend it so it reads as one hand across the years. Send us photos and we'll advise before anything is moved.

What stone should I choose?

It depends on the setting and the regulations, but mostly Portland limestone and Welsh slate — both cut beautifully by hand and weather honestly. We'll talk you through how each looks over decades, what churchyards allow, and which suits your words. There's no single right answer, and we won't pretend there is.

How long does a piece take?

A house name, a few weeks. A headstone, longer — drawing, approval, carving and setting all take real time, and memorials should never be hurried. We'll give you an honest schedule at the start and keep you posted. If there's a date that matters, tell us early and we'll plan for it.

Do you work from anywhere, or just Dorset?

The workshop is on Portland and we set memorials across Dorset and the South West, but lettering commissions and slate pieces travel anywhere — we've posted carved slate across the UK and beyond. For a headstone, distance to the churchyard matters; for a wall piece, it doesn't at all.

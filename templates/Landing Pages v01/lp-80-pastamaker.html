DISH 01 / THE LABORATORIOFLOUR, WATER, PATIENCE

Good pasta is flour, water and patience. Mostly the patience.

We opened Semola in 2016 because the fresh pasta on the shelves near us was neither fresh nor, really, pasta — gummy, over-salted, made somewhere far away weeks before. We wanted the opposite: a room where the dough is mixed at dawn, rested properly, and cut into shape by hand while you can watch through the glass.

Everything starts with the flour. We work in Italian '00' and coarse durum semolina, choosing the blend for the shape — egg dough for silky tagliatelle and stuffed parcels, semolina and water for the southern shapes that want a bit of bite. We draw through bronze dies, never Teflon, because the rough surface is what makes sauce cling instead of slide.

What we don't sell fresh that day, we dry slowly at low temperature — the way it keeps its flavour instead of cooking to cardboard. It's slower and it costs more and it's the entire point. Pasta is a cheap food made precious only by the care you take with it.

What's the difference between your fresh and dried pasta?

Fresh pasta is the egg-rich dough we cut that morning — silky, tender, and cooked in a minute or two; you keep it in the fridge and eat it within a few days. Dried pasta is our semolina dough drawn through bronze dies and dried slowly at low temperature, so it keeps for months in the cupboard and holds a firmer bite. They suit different dishes and sauces, and we're happy to steer you: delicate egg shapes for cream and butter, sturdy bronze shapes for tomato and ragù.

How much pasta should I buy per person?

For fresh pasta as a main course, reckon on roughly 120–150g per adult, a little less if there's a lot of sauce or a starter first. Dried pasta swells more, so 80–100g per person is plenty. We weigh to order at the counter, so tell us how many you're feeding and whether it's a main or a first course and we'll box exactly the right amount — no guessing, no waste.

How do I cook fresh pasta so it doesn't go gluey?

Use a big pot of well-salted, properly boiling water — salty like the sea — and don't crowd it. Fresh pasta cooks fast, often ninety seconds to three minutes depending on the shape, so taste early and often. Drain it a touch before it's done, keep a mug of the starchy water, and finish it in the warm sauce for the last thirty seconds so the two come together. That last step is the secret; boiling then dumping cold sauce on top is where it goes wrong.

Do you cater for egg-free or gluten-free diets?

Yes to egg-free — our semolina-and-water doughs (many of the southern shapes and our bronze-dried range) contain no egg at all, and we label them clearly. Gluten-free is harder to do well with fresh pasta, but we make a small gluten-free range to order with a day's notice; it's a different animal but a good one. Tell us your needs and we'll point you to what works rather than sell you something that won't.

Can I book a pasta class for a group?

Gladly. Classes are two hands-on hours at the bench where you make the dough, roll and shape it, then cook and eat what you made. We run open sessions for individuals and couples, and private bookings for birthdays, hen-dos and work teams. Groups get the run of the rolling benches and a lot of flour everywhere; tell us numbers and any dietary needs and we'll set it up. Everyone leaves able to do it at home.

Do you supply restaurants and delis?

We do, and it's a real part of the business. We'll make fresh and dried pasta to order — your shapes, your fillings, our dough — on a standing weekly delivery so your kitchen always has it. Delis take our dried range and matching sauces for the shelf. Give us a realistic order and lead time and you'll get pasta that makes your menu look like you rolled it yourself.

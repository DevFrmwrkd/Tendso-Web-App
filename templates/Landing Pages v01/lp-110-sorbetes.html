SCOOP 01 / THE CARTNOSTALGIA ON WHEELS

'Dirty' is a joke. There's nothing dirty about it — just the street, and joy.

Manong's Sorbetes is a traditional sorbetes maker, and first, that name: sorbetes is affectionately called "dirty ice cream" — not because it's unclean, but because it's the humble street version, sold from a cart on the corner rather than a shiny parlour, the ice cream of Filipino childhood. The nickname is a badge of honour. There's nothing dirty about it; there's everything nostalgic about it.

What makes sorbetes special is that it's traditionally made with coconut milk (gata) or carabao's milk rather than heavy dairy cream, which gives it a lighter, cleaner, distinctly Filipino character — and flavours that belong to nowhere else: ube (purple yam), keso (yes, cheese, and it's wonderful), mango, chocolate, and thick tsokolate. We churn ours the old way and keep it in the classic drum inside a hand-painted wooden cart, complete with the brass bell that every Filipino recognises from a hundred metres.

We started in 2014 to keep the tradition rolling — literally. Manong pushes the cart through neighbourhoods, parks near schools and plazas, and scoops onto a cone, into a cup, or, for the purists, stuffed inside a soft pan de sal, which is exactly how a lot of us first ate it. And we book the whole cart, manong and all, for parties, weddings, reunions and corporate events — because nothing makes a crowd smile like the bell, the umbrella, and unlimited scoops.

Why is sorbetes called 'dirty ice cream' — is it actually unhygienic?

No — the nickname is affectionate and a little tongue-in-cheek, not a description of hygiene. 'Dirty ice cream' (sorbetes) got the name simply because it's the humble, street-sold version of ice cream: dispensed from a colourful cart pushed around neighbourhoods and parked by schools and plazas, rather than served in a fancy air-conditioned parlour. To generations of Filipinos it's the ice cream of childhood, and the 'dirty' tag is worn almost with pride. In practice we take cleanliness seriously — the ice cream is churned and handled properly, kept cold in the traditional drum, and served with clean scoops and utensils. The name is pure nostalgia and humour; there's nothing dirty about it.

What flavours do you have, and what's this about cheese ice cream?

Our line-up is the classic Filipino sorbetes roster: ube (purple yam), keso (cheese), mango, chocolate and tsokolate (a thicker, richer chocolate). And yes — keso, cheese ice cream, is a real and beloved Filipino flavour, and it tends to be the one that surprises first-timers and then wins them over completely. It's mildly sweet, savoury-edged and creamy, often with little bits of cheese through it, and it works far better than it sounds precisely because sorbetes isn't overly sweet to begin with. Ube is the eternal favourite, mango is bright and summery, and chocolate and tsokolate cover the classic craving. Most people get a mix. For bookings, you choose which flavours you'd like on the cart. If you've never had cheese ice cream, order a scoop and thank us later.

What makes sorbetes different from regular ice cream?

The biggest difference is the base: traditional sorbetes is made with coconut milk (gata) or carabao's (water buffalo) milk rather than the heavy dairy cream used in most commercial ice cream. That gives sorbetes a lighter, cleaner, less heavy texture and a distinct flavour that lets the ube, mango and other flavours come through brightly — it's refreshing on a hot day in a way that rich, dense premium ice cream often isn't. It's typically a little less sweet and less fatty too, which is part of why you can happily eat it in the tropical heat and why the cheese flavour works so well against it. Beyond the recipe, sorbetes is defined by the painted cart, the drum, the bell, and the ways it's served, including in pan de sal. We make ours the traditional coconut-milk way to keep that character intact.

Can I really have it in a bread roll (pan de sal)?

Yes, and honestly you should try it at least once — sorbetes stuffed inside a soft pan de sal is a genuine, much-loved Filipino way of eating it, effectively the original ice cream sandwich, and for a lot of Filipinos it's exactly how they first had it as kids. The slightly sweet, pillowy bread against the cold, creamy scoop is a surprisingly perfect combination: the bread soaks up a little of the melt, it's easy to hold and eat on the go, and it's more filling, which made it a smart, affordable merienda for generations of schoolkids. We happily serve it this way alongside the usual cone and cup, and at bookings it's often the option that gets the biggest delighted reaction. If you've only ever had sorbetes on a cone, ask manong for the pan de sal version.

How do I book the cart for a party, wedding or event?

Just message or call us with your date, location, rough guest count and the kind of event, and we'll take it from there. Booking the cart means you get the whole package: the hand-painted wooden cart, manong to scoop and keep the queue happy, the bell and umbrella, and unlimited scoops of your chosen flavours for guests for an agreed period of time. It's a phenomenally popular choice for kids' birthdays, family reunions, weddings, school fairs, store openings and corporate events, precisely because it delights every age group and doubles as a charming, photogenic centrepiece. We coordinate timing, flavours and headcount with you ahead of the day so there's plenty churned and nobody misses out. Popular dates — weekends, holidays, the December party season — get booked up, so reach out as early as you can.

Do you sell tubs to take home, and where can I find the cart normally?

Yes to both. If you fall for a flavour, we sell tubs of our coconut-milk sorbetes — ube, keso, mango and the rest — to take home for the freezer, perfect for merienda and for unexpected guests; you can order ahead and collect, or add tubs to an event booking. As for finding the cart day to day: manong still does neighbourhood rounds around Marikina and nearby areas, pushing the cart through villages and parking near schools, plazas and village gates, and the classic way to find him is to follow the bell. Because the route moves, the easiest thing is to message us to ask where the cart is that day, or to request a stop on your street. For a specific place and time, a booking is the sure way — but there's a special magic to just hearing that bell down the road.

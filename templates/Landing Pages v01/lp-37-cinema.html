One screen. One film. Chosen like it matters.

The Valiant has shown exactly one film at a time since 1936. No screen seven, no recliner upsell, no twenty minutes of adverts. A programme picked by people who watch everything so you can watch the right thing — with an interval, because some films deserve a breath.

REEL 01 / THE HOUSEGRADE II · BARREL CEILING · 180 SEATS

Ninety years of one screen.

The Valiant opened in 1936 with a newsreel, a cartoon and a western, and it has survived television, video, the multiplex and the streaming era by doing the one thing none of them do: choosing. One film at a time, programmed by people who watch relentlessly, argued over at a Tuesday meeting that has been known to run three hours.

The auditorium seats 180 under the original barrel ceiling. The projection box runs modern digital and — four times a year, to a full house — the restored 35mm projector, which sounds like soft rain and smells faintly of warm metal.

There's an interval in anything over two and a quarter hours. Ice cream arrives in tubs, on trays, from a person. We are aware this is old-fashioned. It is also, by unanimous audience verdict, correct.

REEL 03 / THE ARGUMENTWHY A SINGLE SCREEN SURVIVES

Why we're still here.

EXHIBIT A

Programmed, not scheduled.

A multiplex fills screens; we choose a film. The Tuesday programming meeting is the whole product — everything else is seats and light.

EXHIBIT B

Projection is a craft here.

A trained projectionist checks focus, masking and sound for every single show. Four times a year the 35mm comes out. You can tell. That's the point.

EXHIBIT C

An audience, not footfall.

Intervals, tub ice cream, a bar you can hear yourself in, and no adverts after the lights go down. People come alone here and nobody finds it odd.

REEL 07 / ASKED AT THE BOX OFFICEUSUALLY AT TEN TO EIGHT

Asked at the box office.

Anything else — ring 01584 872 209. During a show the phone is off; that should tell you everything about our priorities.

Why only one film at a time?

Because choosing is the service. Anyone can show you everything — that's called the internet. We watch everything so that what's on our screen is worth leaving the house for. Ninety years in, the method holds.

Do I need to book?

For 35mm nights and anything with "restored" in the title, yes, quickly. For most evening shows, walking up works. Saturday matinées are the gamble; bring a fallback plan or a book.

Is there really an interval?

In anything over about two and a quarter hours, yes. Fifteen minutes, house lights, ice cream in tubs from a tray. Directors' estates have written to thank us. Audiences just queue happily.

Is the cinema accessible?

Step-free from Mill Street to the stalls, six wheelchair spaces with companion seats, a hearing loop, and regular subtitled and relaxed screenings — dates on the programme and the Google profile.

Can I bring children?

To anything their certificate allows, obviously — plus the Saturday film club for under-16s and parent-and-baby Thursdays where crying is expected and shushing is banned.

What's on next week?

Decided on Tuesday, posted by Wednesday: outside the cinema, on the website, and in the butcher's window on King Street. If you know why the butcher's, write in; the archive is incomplete.

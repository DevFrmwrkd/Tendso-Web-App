HEAT 01 / THE FORGEONE SMITH, ONE FIRE

A good knife is a tool you'll hand down, not throw away.

Quench & Temper is one smith and one fire in a stone workshop on the edge of the Peak District. I started forging in 2013 because I was tired of knives designed to be replaced — stamped out by the thousand, glued together, dull in a month and landfill in a year. A knife should be the opposite of that: made once, properly, and kept sharp for a lifetime.

Every blade starts as a bar of steel and is drawn out under the hammer, not ground down from a bought-in blank. I forge in high-carbon and stainless steels chosen for the job, normalise and harden by eye and by colour, temper for the right balance of hardness and toughness, then grind and hand-finish the bevels. The handle is fitted to the tang and shaped to the hand, in stabilised timber, horn or micarta.

You can commission a blade to your grip and your kitchen, buy one of the ready pieces from the rack, or bring an old favourite back to life. And whatever leaves here comes with a standing offer: bring it back whenever it needs it and I'll re-sharpen it for as long as I'm at the anvil. A knife made by hand deserves to be kept by hand.

HEAT 03 / THE DIFFERENCEWHY IT HOLDS AN EDGE

Why it holds an edge.

01 / 03

Forged, not ground from a blank.

The steel is drawn out under the hammer, which aligns the grain and lets me put mass exactly where the blade needs it. A ground-down blank is quicker; it is not the same knife.

02 / 03

Heat-treated by eye.

Hardness is everything in a blade, and I read it by colour and by the steel itself — normalise, harden, quench, temper — so each knife holds an edge and won't chip or bend.

03 / 03

Sharp for a lifetime.

A hand-forged knife isn't a one-time purchase, it's a relationship. Bring it back whenever it dulls and I'll put the edge back on, for as long as I'm working.

HEAT 04 / HOW IT'S MADETALK → FORGE → GRIND → SIGN
  1. STEP 01

    Talk it through

    Tell me what you cook or what you carry, how big your hand is, what you've hated about past knives. We settle on steel, shape, length and handle before a hammer falls.

  2. STEP 02

    Forged to shape

    I draw the blade out under the hammer, forge the bevels, then normalise, harden and temper the steel — the slow heat-treating that decides whether a knife is any good.

  3. STEP 03

    Ground & handled

    The bevels are ground and hand-finished, the handle fitted to the tang and shaped to your grip in your chosen material, then the whole knife is polished and sharpened.

  4. STEP 04

    Tested & signed

    Every blade is edge-tested, balanced in the hand and stamped with the maker's mark before it goes in its sheath or roll — and it comes back to me for sharpening, always.

What's the difference between carbon and stainless steel?

High-carbon steel takes and holds a keener edge and is easier to sharpen to a razor, which is why many chefs and blade lovers prefer it — but it will rust and patina if you leave it wet, so it needs a wipe-down and the odd bit of oil. Stainless steel is far more forgiving of neglect and won't stain, at the cost of being a touch harder to bring to the very finest edge. Neither is "better"; it depends on how much care you'll give it. I'll talk you through it honestly and forge whichever suits how you actually live — there's no point making you a high-maintenance blade if you'll leave it in the sink.

How long does a commission take, and what does it cost?

A single commissioned knife typically takes several weeks to a few months depending on the queue and the complexity — I'm one person and one fire, so I don't rush heat-treating for anybody. Cost depends on the steel, the size, the handle material and any engraving; a straightforward kitchen knife starts lower, a large set or an exotic-handled piece more. I'll give you a clear quote before anything starts and a realistic timeline, and I'd always rather take longer and get it right than hand you something I'm not proud to sign.

Can I really bring it back for sharpening forever?

Yes, and I mean it — anything that leaves this forge comes back for sharpening free of charge for as long as I'm at the anvil. A hand-forged knife is meant to be used hard, and a used knife dulls; that's not a fault, it's a knife doing its job. Bring it in, leave it a few days, collect it sharp. I sharpen on stones by hand, not a powered grinder that eats the blade, so your knife keeps its life instead of losing millimetres every visit. I'll also happily sharpen knives from other makers.

How do I look after a hand-forged knife?

Wash it by hand and dry it straight away — never the dishwasher, which will wreck the edge and the handle. Keep carbon steel wiped dry and give it a wipe of food-safe oil now and then; a little patina is normal and actually protects the steel, so don't panic when it darkens. Cut on wood or plastic, never glass, stone or a steel board, which will destroy the edge in seconds. Store it in the sheath, a block or on a magnetic strip rather than loose in a drawer. Do that and bring it back when it dulls, and it'll outlive all of us.

Do you sell ready-made knives, or only commissions?

Both. There's usually a rack of finished pieces you can handle and buy on the day — kitchen knives, outdoor blades and the odd one-off I made to try a steel or a shape — which is the fastest way to own one and a good way to feel the work before you commission. If nothing on the rack is quite right, we commission to your grip and your needs. Ready pieces come with the same sharpening-for-life offer as commissions, so you're not getting a lesser knife by buying off the rack.

Can I commission a knife as a gift?

Gladly, and they make extraordinary gifts — a chef's knife for someone who cooks, a bushcraft blade for someone who's outdoors, a wedding or anniversary piece engraved with a date or name. If you're not sure of the exact spec, a gift voucher lets the recipient design their own with me, which many people prefer for something this personal. I can engrave, box and, for a forging-day gift, put them at the anvil to make it themselves. Just give me a realistic lead time before the occasion — good steel can't be hurried.

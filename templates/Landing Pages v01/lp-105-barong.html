STITCH 01 / THE ATELIERTAAL, THE HOME OF EMBROIDERY

A barong is not sewn in a day, and it is not worn out in a lifetime.

Casa Piña is a barong Tagalog atelier in Taal, Batangas — a town whose name has meant fine embroidery for generations. The barong Tagalog is the Filipino national dress: a formal shirt, worn untucked over an undershirt, traditionally in cloth so fine it is almost sheer, ornamented not with print but with embroidery worked directly into the fabric. Made properly, it is one of the most elegant garments in the world. Made carelessly, it is a costume. We only make it properly.

The atelier was founded in 1962 and has always worked the same way: cloth chosen by hand, measurements taken in person, patterns cut to the individual body, and the embroidery — the calado openwork and the pechera front panel — stitched by hand by our manunuburan, the embroiderers whose craft is Taal's inheritance. We work in the classic barong fabrics: piña, woven from pineapple-leaf fibre, the finest and most prized; piña-seda, blended with silk for a little more forgiveness; and jusi, a smooth, more affordable woven cloth for barong worn often.

We dress grooms and their entourages, ninongs and principal sponsors, graduates, officials at their inaugurals, and anyone who wants to own a real barong rather than a printed imitation. We also restore and care for heirloom barong — the ones handed down, yellowed or torn — because a hand-made barong is meant to outlive the occasion it was made for. Come and be measured; wear the national dress as it should be worn.

What's the difference between piña, piña-seda and jusi?

They are the three classic barong fabrics, and choosing between them is the first real decision of any commission. Piña is woven from the fibre of pineapple leaves — extraordinarily fine, slightly stiff, faintly golden, almost translucent, and the most prized and expensive cloth for a barong; it is what you'd choose for a wedding or the most important occasions, and it ages beautifully. Piña-seda is piña blended with silk (seda), which keeps much of piña's elegance while being a little softer, more forgiving and somewhat more affordable — a lovely middle ground. Jusi is a smooth, fine woven cloth, more affordable and hard-wearing, ideal for a barong you'll wear more often to work functions and everyday formal occasions. All three are worn untucked over an undershirt and all can carry hand embroidery; the difference is in fineness, sheerness, drape, longevity and price. We'll show you all of them and never push you towards more than the day requires.

Is the embroidery really done by hand?

Yes — the calado and pechera on our barong are embroidered by hand, by our own manunuburan (embroiderers), in Taal, which is precisely the craft this town has been known for across generations. Machine embroidery and printed 'barong' can imitate the look from a distance, but up close the difference is unmistakable: hand calado is drawn-thread openwork, where threads are actually withdrawn from the weave and the remaining ones stitched into lacy patterns, and the hand-worked pechera has a depth, irregularity and life that a machine simply cannot reproduce. It is slow, exacting, eye-straining work, and it is the single biggest reason a proper barong takes weeks to make and costs what it does. We're glad to show you hand embroidery beside machine work so you can see for yourself before you decide.

How long does a made-to-measure barong take?

Plan on several weeks from measurement to final fitting for a standard commission, and longer for elaborate hand embroidery or a large wedding entourage — hand work cannot be rushed without ruining exactly what you're paying for. The timeline covers the consultation and measuring, cutting your pattern, the making of the barong, and then the embroidery, which is the longest and most delicate stage, followed by a fitting and any adjustments before delivery. For weddings, graduations and inaugurals — which cluster into busy seasons — we strongly advise coming to us as early as you can, ideally a couple of months ahead for an entourage, both so the work is unhurried and so we can secure the cloth you want. If you're in a genuine hurry we'll tell you honestly what's achievable; we'd rather turn away a rush than deliver a barong that isn't up to our name.

Can you dress a whole wedding entourage?

Yes, and it's some of our favourite work — coordinating barong for the groom, the fathers, the ninongs and the groomsmen, matched in cloth and embroidery pattern but each cut and fitted to the individual man, and all delivered in time for fittings before the day. There's a real art to making a set look unified on the day while flattering very different bodies. We'll usually recommend a slightly more forgiving cloth like piña-seda or jusi for a large entourage where budget and wearability across many men matter, and reserve the finest piña for the groom, though it's entirely your choice. The key is time: bring us the party, the date and the vision as early as possible, ideally a couple of months out, and we'll manage measurements, cloth, embroidery and fittings so it comes together without stress. We coordinate ladies' Filipiniana and kimona for the women of the party too.

Do you restore or repair old and heirloom barong?

Yes — restoration and heirloom care is a genuine specialty and among the most meaningful work we do. A hand-made barong is designed to outlive the occasion it was made for, which means many families have a lolo's or father's barong put away — yellowed with age, weakened at the folds, or torn at the delicate pechera — that they long to wear again, often to a wedding. Depending on the cloth and the damage, we can gently clean and de-yellow, stabilise and mend weakened fabric, and re-embroider or repair damaged calado and pechera to blend with the original, always assessing honestly first and telling you plainly if a piece is too fragile to safely wear or better conserved than reworked. There are few things more moving than a groom married in his grandfather's restored barong. Bring it in or send photographs of the whole garment and the damage.

How do I care for a barong so it lasts?

Gently, and it will last for decades. Fine barong cloth — especially piña — is delicate, so the golden rules are: wear an undershirt (the traditional camiseta) so body oils and sweat reach it rather than the barong; avoid perfume and deodorant contact with the cloth; and never wad it up or store it on a wire hanger. For cleaning, hand-washing or specialist dry-cleaning as the fabric dictates is far safer than a rough machine wash, and we give specific care guidance with every barong. Store it clean, dry and flat or on a padded hanger, ideally wrapped in acid-free tissue and away from damp, which yellows and weakens the fibre — a real consideration in our climate. Press with care and low heat, or let us press it. And when a barong does age or suffer damage, don't discard it — bring it back. It was made to be repaired and handed down.

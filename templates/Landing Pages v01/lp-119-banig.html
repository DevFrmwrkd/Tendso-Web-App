WEFT 01 / THE COLLECTIVEBASEY, THE BANIG TOWN

Before there were beds, there was the banig. It's still the coolest thing to sleep on.

Banig ni Inay is a weavers' collective in Basey, Samar, a town whose name is practically a synonym for banig — the handwoven Filipino mat. A banig isn't a rug; it's a sleeping mat, rolled out on the floor at night and rolled up in the morning, and for countless Filipino families it's what generations have slept on. In our climate it makes perfect sense: woven grass breathes, stays cool against the skin, and packs away to nothing.

Ours are woven from tikog, a wild sedge grass that grows in the marshes around Samar. The work starts in the water: harvesting the tikog, drying it in the sun, flattening it, dyeing the strands in strong colours, and only then weaving — over and under, strand by strand, on the floor, by hand. A full sleeping mat takes weeks. The bold geometric patterns and figures are Waray designs, learned from mothers and grandmothers, and every weaver's hand is slightly, recognisably her own.

The collective exists so the weavers keep the value of their work. We sell direct — no middlemen taking the margin from women who spent three weeks on a mat — and we ship nationwide. Beyond the classic sleeping banig we weave table runners, placemats, bags, wall pieces and made-to-order sizes for homes, designers and hotels. Buy one and you're not buying décor; you're buying weeks of a woman's skill, and helping keep a Samar craft alive.

What exactly is a banig, and is it a rug?

A banig is a traditional handwoven Filipino mat, and no, it isn't really a rug in the Western sense — its classic use is as a sleeping mat, unrolled on the floor or on a wooden bed at night and rolled up and stored away in the morning. For generations of Filipino families, especially in the provinces, the banig is simply what you sleep on, and it makes a great deal of sense in a tropical climate: woven grass breathes, stays notably cool against the skin, doesn't trap heat the way a mattress does, and takes up almost no space when rolled. Beyond sleeping, banig weaving is also used for table runners, placemats, bags and wall décor, which is where a lot of people first encounter it today. You can absolutely use a banig decoratively, but if you want the authentic experience, unroll one and sleep on it on a hot night.

What is tikog, and why does the material matter?

Tikog is a wild sedge grass that grows in the marshy wetlands of Samar, and it's the traditional material for the banig our region is famous for. It matters enormously, because the quality of a mat begins long before anyone starts weaving. The stalks have to be harvested at the right maturity, dried properly in the sun (under-dried tikog goes mouldy; over-dried gets brittle), flattened, and then dyed — traditionally in strong, saturated colours that hold up over the years. Only then can weaving begin. Well-prepared tikog is pliable, strong, smooth against the skin and takes dye beautifully; poorly prepared fibre gives you a mat that's rough, weak or fades quickly. We handle the whole chain ourselves here in Samar precisely because that control is what makes the difference. Other regions use pandan or buri, each with their own character; tikog is ours.

Why does a banig cost what it does, and how long does one take?

Because it's weeks of skilled human labour, not a manufactured product. A full-size sleeping banig typically takes a weaver a matter of weeks to complete, working strand by strand, over and under, by hand — and that's after the days spent harvesting, drying, flattening and dyeing the tikog. Smaller pieces like runners and placemats take days rather than weeks. Then there's the skill itself: the bold geometric patterns and figures are worked in as the weaving progresses, which demands genuine expertise learned over years, usually from a mother or grandmother. When you understand that, the price of a good banig stops looking like the price of a mat and starts looking like what it is: fair payment for a substantial piece of one woman's time and a lifetime of accumulated skill.

How do I care for a banig so it lasts?

Banig is durable and improves with use, but it's a natural fibre so a little sense goes a long way. Keep it dry — this is the main rule, because prolonged damp or humidity causes mould and weakens the fibres. If it gets wet, dry it thoroughly and promptly, ideally in the air but out of harsh, prolonged direct sunlight, which fades the dyes over time. For cleaning, wipe it down with a barely damp cloth and let it dry fully; don't soak, machine-wash or scrub it hard. Store it rolled rather than sharply folded, since repeated hard creases will eventually crack the strands, and keep it somewhere ventilated rather than sealed in plastic in a humid cupboard. Treated this way a banig comfortably lasts years and gets softer with use; if it frays at an edge, talk to us — small repairs are often possible.

Can I order custom sizes, colours or patterns?

Yes — made-to-order is a big part of what we do, for individuals and for interior designers, resorts and hotels. Because every piece is woven by hand from fibre we dye ourselves, we can work to your dimensions, your colour palette and either traditional Waray patterns or a design worked out with you, including matched sets for a project. The honest constraint is time: hand-weaving cannot be rushed, so a custom order — particularly a large mat or a volume order of matched pieces — needs a realistic lead time, and we'll always tell you truthfully how long it will take rather than promise something we can't deliver. We'll also advise on what works: certain patterns suit certain sizes, some colours hold better over years of use. Send us your dimensions, intended use and any references, and we'll come back with what's possible, a timeline and a fair price.

Does buying direct really help the weavers?

Yes, and it's the entire reason the collective exists. The long-standing problem in traditional craft is that the person doing the skilled, time-consuming work often captures the smallest share of the final price, with middlemen and resellers taking the margin — which is precisely how crafts die out, because younger people quite reasonably decline to spend three weeks weaving for a fraction of what the mat sells for. By organising as a collective and selling direct to customers, wholesale buyers and trade clients, we cut those layers out so that the woman who actually wove your banig is paid properly for her weeks of work. That has a real, visible effect: weavers stay in the craft, they can afford to teach it to daughters and neighbours, and the patterns and knowledge that would otherwise be lost keep being passed on.

LOT 01 / THE SHOPCASTOR & POLLUX, GRINDING

Two ingredients. Three days. One window.

Ursa exists because of one bar of Madagascan chocolate eaten in 2016 that tasted of raspberries despite containing none. Chasing that flavour led to a roaster, a vintage winnower rebuilt from Dutch scrap, two granite melangers named Castor and Pollux, and eventually this shop.

Bean-to-bar means exactly that: sacks arrive, bars leave, everything happens in between the two — behind the big window, where you can watch. Most of our bars are two ingredients, cacao and sugar. The flavour differences between origins do the rest, and they are not subtle.

The bonbon counter runs Fridays and Saturdays, made fresh that week and gone by Sunday. We refuse to ship them; a bonbon that can survive the post isn't a bonbon worth eating.

LOT 03 / THE POINTPRINTED INSIDE EVERY WRAPPER

Why bother doing it this way.

REASON 1/3

We make it. Actually.

Not melted, not remoulded, not "crafted" from someone else's couverture. Sacks of cacao arrive; bars leave. The window shows the whole process because there's nothing to hide.

REASON 2/3

Origins you can taste.

Madagascar tastes of red fruit, Vietnam of honey and spice, Ecuador of flowers. Same recipe, wildly different bars. That's the entire argument for doing this properly.

REASON 3/3

Freshness is an ingredient.

Bonbons live a week and we sell them that way. Bars are stamped with their grind date. Chocolate is produce, not confectionery with a two-year shelf life.

LOT 07 / ASKED AT THE COUNTERMID-TASTING, USUALLY

Asked at the counter.

Anything else — ring 01865 552 384 or message us. During tempering, answers arrive slightly delayed and smelling of chocolate.

Why does your chocolate cost more than supermarket chocolate?

Because we pay farmers three to four times the commodity rate for cacao worth naming, then spend three days making it into chocolate. A bar costs about the same as two coffees and lasts considerably longer, unless it doesn't.

What does "bean-to-bar" actually mean?

That we make chocolate from raw cacao, not melt down someone else's. Roasting, cracking, winnowing, grinding, conching, tempering — all of it happens behind the window. Most "artisan chocolate" skips all of that; ask any maker where their sacks are.

Which bar should I start with?

Come taste and we'll find out together. The general pattern: fruit people land on Madagascar, caramel people on Venezuela, and people who claim not to like dark chocolate get the Ecuador 70% and go quiet.

Why won't you ship bonbons?

Fresh cream ganache lives about a week and hates temperature swings. A bonbon engineered to survive the post is engineered away from being good. Bars ship happily; bonbons are a reason to visit Jericho.

Do you have vegan and dairy-free bars?

Most of the shelf already is — dark bars are cacao and sugar, nothing else. The oat-milk bars are properly creamy, and Friday's counter always has vegan bonbons, labelled and unpatronising.

Can I see it being made?

The window runs whenever the machines do — roasting Tuesdays, grinding midweek, moulding Thursdays. Tastings go behind the scenes properly, melangers and all. Children ask the best questions; bring them.

CELL 01 / THE APIARYPENYLAN ORCHARD, ABOVE THE WYE

Twelve hives, counted daily.

The apiary sits in a south-facing orchard above the Wye: twelve hives, each named after the storm blowing the week it was built. Aurora is gentle. Bronagh holds grudges. Callum produced forty kilos last July and has been insufferable since.

We extract cold, jar raw and never blend across hives — which means jars vary by hive, by month, by what the valley offered. April's is pale and floral off the orchard blossom; July's is dark and muscular off the bramble and lime. The label tells you everything because the label is the point.

The rest of the year is swarm rescue, courses for new keepers, and explaining to visitors that the bees are not angry, they are busy, and the difference matters.

CELL 03 / THE CREEDWRITTEN IN WAX

The keeper's creed.

1

Raw means actually raw.

Cold-extracted, coarse-filtered, jarred once. Never heated, never blended, never "produce of more than one country". If it crystallises, that's honey behaving.

2

Traceable to the hive.

Every jar names its hive, its fortnight and its forage. When you taste the difference between Aurora's April and Callum's July, you'll stop buying anonymous honey.

3

The bees set the pace.

Some months there's no honey to sell and we say so. A sold-out shelf is the apiary working correctly — scarcity is the certificate.

CELL 07 / ASKED AT THE GATEUSUALLY MID-VEIL

Asked at the gate.

Anything else — ring 01600 715 224. If we're mid-inspection, the bees have the floor; we'll ring back by evening.

Why does your honey crystallise?

Because it's raw. Real honey sets — the speed depends on the forage. Warm the jar gently in water if you want it runny again, or eat it set on hot toast, which is frankly the superior experience.

Why is each jar different?

Because each hive forages differently and each fortnight flowers differently. Blending would make them identical and boring — the variation is the entire product. Read the label; taste the valley.

Is the hive experience safe? I'm nervous.

Full suits, gentle colonies chosen for visits, and a keeper beside you the whole time. Nervous is normal and usually gone within ten minutes — the hive's calm is contagious. Stings are rare; allergy questions asked at booking, seriously.

There's a swarm in my garden — what do I do?

Nothing but keep your distance and ring us. A swarm is bees between homes, gorged on honey and about as docile as they ever get. Free collection within fifteen miles, usually same day.

Can I keep bees in my garden?

Probably — bees need less space than people think and more attention than they hope. Do the course first; the most expensive mistake in beekeeping is enthusiasm without husbandry, and we've made it so you don't have to.

When is honey available?

When the bees say so — typically June to September, sold by the month until each batch is gone. The sold-out months are not a supply problem; they're how single-apiary honey works.

PLATE 01 / THE INASALBACOLOD, THE HOME OF IT

The secret is the baste. Annatto oil, over the coals, again and again.

Inasal sa Uling is a chicken inasal house in Bacolod City, and inasal is Bacolod's gift to the Filipino table — chicken marinated and grilled over charcoal in a way that belongs to Negros the way lechon belongs to Cebu. "Inasal" means grilled, and "sa uling" means over charcoal, which is the whole point: not roasted, not fried, but grilled over glowing coconut coals so the marinade caramelises and the skin turns amber and smoky.

We opened in 2009 doing one thing properly. Every piece — the pecho breast, the paa leg, the pak-pak wing, and the prized parts like isol, atay and baticolon — is marinated overnight in a blend of lemongrass, calamansi, vinegar, garlic, ginger and annatto, then skewered on bamboo and grilled over coals while we baste it with annatto oil, over and over, until it glows. That basting is the secret, and it's why inasal looks and tastes like nothing else off a grill.

It comes the way it should: with garlic rice, a little saucer of chicken oil to pour over, sinamak — spiced coconut vinegar — to dip and spike it, and your hands, because inasal is kamayan food. Unli rice keeps everyone full. We grill for dine-in, for take-out, and for parties by the tray. Bacolod is the City of Smiles, and there's a reason half of them happen over a plate of inasal.

What makes Bacolod chicken inasal different?

Two things, mainly: the marinade and the baste. Inasal is not simply grilled chicken — it's chicken marinated in a distinctly Negrense blend of lemongrass (tanglad), calamansi, vinegar, garlic, ginger and, crucially, annatto (achuete), then grilled over charcoal while being basted repeatedly with annatto-infused oil. That basting is what gives inasal its signature amber-orange colour and its rich, savoury, faintly tangy flavour that goes well beyond ordinary grilled chicken. It's cooked over live coconut charcoal, which adds smoke and char you can't get from gas or an oven. And it's traditionally served with garlic rice, a saucer of chicken oil, and sinamak for dipping. Bacolod is considered the home of inasal, and eating it here, grilled to order over real uling, is a different thing entirely from the frozen, reheated version.

What is sinamak and chicken oil, and how do I use them?

They're the two things that turn good inasal into a proper Bacolod meal. Chicken oil is the golden, annatto-tinted oil rendered and infused during grilling — you pour it over your garlic rice, and it carries all the smoky, savoury flavour of the grill into every grain. Sinamak is a spiced native vinegar, usually coconut vinegar steeped with siling labuyo, garlic and ginger — sharp, garlicky and hot, used as a dip for the chicken and to cut through the richness. The classic move is to mix a little chicken oil into your rice, dip your inasal in sinamak, and alternate bites. There's no wrong way, but if you've never had inasal 'dressed' properly, ask us and we'll show you — it's the difference between eating grilled chicken and eating inasal.

Should I really eat inasal with my hands?

Yes, if you want the full experience — inasal is classic kamayan food, eaten with clean hands rather than cutlery, and there's a real reason beyond tradition. Eating with your hands lets you get right into the chicken around the bone, mix your rice with chicken oil and sinamak the way you like it, and enjoy it more directly; many Bacolodnons will tell you inasal just tastes better this way. We provide everything you need to do it comfortably and cleanly, and of course cutlery is always there for anyone who prefers it — no pressure at all. But if you're up for it, wash up, dig in with your hands, and eat the way inasal has been enjoyed here for generations.

What are the inasal 'specials' like isol and atay?

The specials are the grilled chicken parts beyond the usual breast and leg, and for a lot of regulars they're the best part of the meal. Isol is the chicken tail — fatty, rich and incredibly flavourful grilled over coals with the annatto baste, and often the first to sell out. Atay is chicken liver, skewered and grilled until just done, savoury and a little creamy. Baticolon is the gizzard, chewy in the good way. Pak-pak (wings) are a favourite for the crispy, well-basted skin. These are all grilled on bamboo skewers and are cheap, punchy and perfect with an ice-cold drink and rice. If you're new to inasal, order a plate of mixed specials for the table — and come a little early, because the isol especially goes fast.

Can you cater a party or grill by the tray?

Yes — party trays and catering are a big part of what we do, because inasal is celebration food and a fixture at Bacolod fiestas, birthdays and office gatherings. We grill by the tray, mixing parts and specials however you like, and pack rice, chicken oil and sinamak on the side so everything arrives ready to dress and eat. We can deliver hot around Bacolod or have trays ready for pick-up, and for bigger events we can talk through quantities. We ask for at least a day's notice for trays, and more during fiesta season and Christmas, so we can marinate properly overnight and grill fresh. Tell us the headcount, the date and the mix of parts — or let us suggest a spread — and we'll sort a fair price.

Do you use real charcoal, and is it grilled fresh?

Yes on both counts — 'sa uling' literally means 'over charcoal,' and we wouldn't call ourselves that if we cut the corner. We grill over live coconut charcoal, which burns hot and clean and gives inasal its essential smoke and char, and we grill to order rather than pre-cooking batches to sit under a lamp. That means when it's busy there's sometimes a short wait, and we'd honestly rather you wait a few minutes for inasal grilled fresh and basted properly than hand you something reheated. The marinating happens the night before — that can't be rushed — but the actual grilling and basting is done fresh for your order. For take-out and trays, ordering ahead means it's grilled to your pick-up or delivery time and travels at its best.
